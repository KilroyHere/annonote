Why Rivers Meander

A short explainer · 6 min read

A river that runs straight for long is the exception. Almost every river on a gentle slope begins to bend, and once a bend forms it tends to grow. The outside of each curve erodes while the inside collects sand and gravel, so the loop slowly migrates across the valley floor.

The reason is a small helical current. As water rounds a bend, it is pushed toward the outer bank, which raises the water surface there. Near the bed, where friction slows the flow, that pressure difference drives water back toward the inner bank, carrying sediment with it.

Over decades the loops become so tight that the river cuts across the narrow neck between them during a flood. The abandoned loop becomes an oxbow lake, and the process starts again downstream.

Engineers once straightened many rivers to speed up shipping. Today, restoration projects often put the bends back, because meanders slow floodwater and create habitats that straight channels cannot.
